Perimenopause: symptoms, causes and treatment options

Perimenopause is the stretch of years before your periods stop for good. Hormones rise and fall unevenly, and that can show up as anything from heavier periods to 3 am wake-ups. Here is what is happening and what you can do about it.

JWWritten by Jenna Whitaker, FNP-C, MSCP ✓ Medically reviewed by Dr. Natalie Brooks, MD, FACOG Last reviewed Oct 14, 2026 6 sources cited

The short answer

  • Perimenopause usually begins in your 40s and can last anywhere from a few years to about ten.
  • Irregular periods, hot flashes, poor sleep and mood changes are the most common signs.
  • Diagnosis is mostly based on your age, cycle and symptoms. Blood tests help rule out other causes.
  • Treatment ranges from lifestyle changes to non-hormonal medicine to hormone therapy. The right fit depends on you.

What is perimenopause?

Perimenopause, also called the menopausal transition, is the time when your ovaries gradually make less estrogen and progesterone. The change is not smooth. Levels can spike higher than usual one month and drop low the next, which is why symptoms often come and go.

It ends at menopause, which is confirmed once you have gone 12 months in a row without a period. In the United States the average age of menopause is about 51, so most women notice perimenopause sometime in their 40s. Some notice it in their late 30s.

Common symptoms

Every woman's experience is different. Some have few symptoms, others feel like their body changed overnight. These are the ones we hear about most often in our Farmington clinic:

  • Irregular periods
  • Heavier bleeding
  • Hot flashes
  • Night sweats
  • Trouble sleeping
  • Mood changes and anxiety
  • Brain fog
  • Vaginal dryness
  • Lower sex drive
  • Weight gain around the middle
  • Bladder changes
  • Joint aches

Many of these overlap with other conditions, such as thyroid disease, iron deficiency or depression. That overlap is exactly why it is worth getting checked rather than assuming.

Causes and risk factors

Perimenopause is a normal part of aging, caused by the natural decline in the number of eggs in your ovaries. A few things can make it start earlier or feel more intense:

  • Smoking, which is linked to earlier menopause
  • A family history of early menopause
  • Cancer treatment such as chemotherapy or pelvic radiation
  • Hysterectomy, even when the ovaries are kept
  • Some autoimmune conditions

How it's evaluated

There is no single test that confirms perimenopause. Your provider puts the picture together from a few pieces:

  1. Your storyYour age, how your cycle has changed and which symptoms bother you most. A simple symptom diary helps.
  2. A focused examBlood pressure, weight and a pelvic exam if you are due or having bleeding changes.
  3. Tests to rule things outBlood work for thyroid function, iron levels, or pregnancy when symptoms could have another cause.
  4. A shared planWe talk through what we found and which options make sense for you.

Treatment options

You do not have to "just get through it." There are good options, and most women use more than one. Here is how they compare:

Hormone therapy

Estrogen, often with progesterone, is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats. It is a good fit for many healthy women under 60, and not right for everyone.

Non-hormonal options

Several prescription medicines can ease hot flashes, sleep problems and mood symptoms for women who can't or prefer not to use hormones.

Lifestyle medicine

Strength training, steady sleep habits, less alcohol and a protein-forward diet can make a real difference, alone or alongside medicine.

Build a plan

Hormone therapy is one option, not the answer to every symptom. The right approach depends on your symptoms, medical history, risk factors and what matters to you.

When to see a provider

Perimenopause is normal, but some changes need a closer look. Book a visit if you notice:

  • Bleeding that soaks a pad or tampon every hour for several hours
  • Periods closer than 21 days apart, or bleeding after sex
  • Any bleeding after you have gone 12 months without a period
  • Symptoms that affect your sleep, work or relationships
  • Low mood that lasts most days for two weeks or more

Perimenopause FAQ

At what age does perimenopause usually start?

Most women notice changes in their 40s, though some start in their late 30s. It often lasts several years and ends at menopause, which in the United States happens at an average age of about 51.

Can a blood test tell me if I am in perimenopause?

Usually not on its own. Hormone levels swing from day to day during perimenopause, so a single test can look normal. Providers mostly rely on your age, cycle changes and symptoms, and use blood tests to rule out other causes such as thyroid disease or iron deficiency.

Can I still get pregnant during perimenopause?

Yes. Ovulation becomes less predictable but does not stop until menopause. If you do not want to become pregnant, keep using birth control until a provider confirms menopause.

Is hormone therapy safe for perimenopause symptoms?

For many healthy women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause, the benefits of hormone therapy for bothersome symptoms outweigh the risks. It is not right for everyone. Your personal and family history decide whether it fits, and non-hormonal options are available.

How do I know when perimenopause is over?

Menopause is confirmed after 12 months in a row without a period, when there is no other cause. The time after that point is called postmenopause.
